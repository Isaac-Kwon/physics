Silicon is a **semiconductor**: at room temperature it holds very few free charge carriers. A particle crossing a thin wafer frees roughly 80 electron–hole pairs per micrometre of path. A wafer 100 µm thick yields several thousand pairs, enough to read out with low-noise electronics.

A reverse-biased p–n junction sweeps the freed carriers away before they recombine. They arrive at electrodes, and the small current pulse is the signal.

Cut the electrodes into strips or squares (**pixels**) and the pulse tells you where the particle passed, to within microns. A wafer a fraction of a millimetre thick is also nearly transparent to a fast particle, which loses little energy and direction.

Silicon is fast and compact, but it is damaged by heavy radiation.
