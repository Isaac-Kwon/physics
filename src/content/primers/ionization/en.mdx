A fast charged particle passing through matter pushes on the electrons of the atoms it passes. Sometimes it knocks one loose. The atom becomes an ion and a free electron remains. This is **ionization**.

Each knock costs the particle only a tiny fraction of its energy, but along a path of centimetres it happens thousands of times. The result is a thin trail of freed electrons and ions that follows the particle's path.

Detectors turn this trail into a signal: collect the charge with an electric field, or read the light from atoms that relax afterwards.

The number of electrons freed per unit length is the **energy loss** dE/dx. It depends on the particle's charge and speed, and hardly at all on how it was produced. Slower particles deposit more per centimetre. The loss reaches a minimum at moderately relativistic speed and then rises only slowly.
